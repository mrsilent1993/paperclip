# "What if..." candidate episodes, 1700–1914 (non-Vietnam): turning points, ordinary narrators, contrarian twists

Scope note: 10 candidates, worldwide, 1700–1914. Vietnam/Indochina excluded, as are the topics the channel already used (Library of Alexandria, Black Death, Tambora/1816, Titanic, Semmelweis, War of 1812). Labels: **[FACT]** means sourced and broadly agreed. **[DEBATE]** means scholars or sources disagree. **[SPEC]** means the channel's own counterfactual speculation. Research ran under a tool budget of about 16 searches, so many facts rest on reputable secondary sources (NOAA, NPS, BnF, Smithsonian, National Geographic, USNI, Science History Institute, Rothschild Archive) rather than primary documents. Each candidate's "Gaps" lists what still needs checking before scripting.

## Key Question 1: Which turning points hinge on a delayed message, chance, weather, disease, or one ordinary person's decision?

### Takeaway
The strongest candidates are ones where information failed to arrive or was ignored: Cuban hurricane warnings banned in 1900, a Dutch warning about Perry in 1852 dismissed as "dubious", a volcano commission's "no danger" report printed on the morning of the eruption, and a Waterloo victory dispatch that outran the government. A second group turns on disease rather than battle: mosquitoes in Haiti → Louisiana, lice in Napoleon's 1812 retreat, scurvy in the Royal Navy.

### Cited Findings

#### Candidate 1: The News from Waterloo (June 1815). Did a banker "steal" Britain with a carrier pigeon?
- **Real event [FACT]:** Wellington's victory at Waterloo (18 June 1815). Journalism professor Brian Cathcart (*The News From Waterloo*) found that Nathan Rothschild was in England, not at Waterloo. Early word of victory was being "told freely in the City from the morning of Wednesday 21 June". Cathcart traces the earliest official news to Wellington's dispatch carried by messenger to Lord Bathurst — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/story/where-do-anti-semitic-conspiracy-theories-about-the-rothschild-family-come-from); [Rothschild Archive PDF](https://www.rothschildarchive.org/materials/nathan_and_waterloo.pdf)
- The Rothschild Archive says Rothschild couriers brought news "a full 48 hours before the government's own riders" reached Downing Street. It also says the evidence for a large profit is "slender" and that it holds no records to measure any gain — [Rothschild Archive](https://rothschildarchive.org/business/n_m_rothschild_and_sons_london/nathan_mayer_rothschild_and_the_waterloo_commission)
- **Origin of the myth [FACT]:** Cathcart traced the "made a fortune on Waterloo" story to an 1846 antisemitic pamphlet, *Histoire édifiante et curieuse de Rothschild Ier, roi des juifs* — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/story/where-do-anti-semitic-conspiracy-theories-about-the-rothschild-family-come-from)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the Channel weather or the courier had delayed the news by three more days? Would markets, or the politics of peace, have moved differently?
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief says one insider used secret information to make a killing, and that "information is power". Evidence says the story was invented 31 years later as propaganda, and the real "fast news" travelled by ordinary messengers and gossip in the City. The twist is that the most famous "insider trading" story in history is itself fake news. **[FACT]** for the pamphlet origin. **[DEBATE]** for exactly how much Rothschild gained, which the Archive says cannot be measured.
- **Ordinary POV:** a Channel boatman, a post-chaise courier, or a junior clerk at the Stock Exchange hearing rumours on 21 June.
- **Visceral detail:** GAP. "Waterloo teeth" (dentures made from dead soldiers' teeth) is a popular claim that this session did not verify. Check a museum source, e.g. the Science Museum, before use.
- **Communication angle:** London waited roughly 3 days for news of a battle about 300 km away. Couriers, ships and the weather decided who knew first.
- **Then-and-now:** high-frequency trading and latency arbitrage; viral conspiracy theories that outlive their debunking.
- **Sensitivity flag:** the myth is antisemitic in origin. The episode must frame it as debunking a conspiracy theory and must never repeat the claim uncritically.

#### Candidate 2: Galveston hurricane (8 September 1900). The warning that was banned.
- **Real event [FACT]:** Galveston, Texas, was struck on 8 September 1900. Estimated deaths range from 8,000 to 12,000, the deadliest US natural disaster — [Earth Magazine](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-september-8-1900-massive-hurricane-strikes-galveston-texas); [NOAA AOML](https://www.aoml.noaa.gov/hurricane_blog/115th-anniversary-of-the-great-galveston-hurricane)
- **The banned warnings [FACT, details DEBATE]:** In late August 1900 the US War Department/Weather Bureau blocked weather telegrams from Cuban observatories, citing tensions after the Spanish–American War. US forecasters expected the storm to curve northeast. Cuban forecasters said it would continue west toward Texas — [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/hurricane-tracking-forecasting-advances); [Fox/AP](https://www.foxnews.com/story/1900-hurricane-changed-galveston-forecasting); [Face2Face Africa](https://face2faceafrica.com/article/how-the-infamous-galveston-hurricane-killed-12000-after-the-u-s-played-politics-with-weather-forecasting-in-1900/amp) (weaker outlet)
- Local office manager Isaac Cline raised storm flags on 7 September. His later claim to have ridden the beach warning residents is questioned by Erik Larson's *Isaac's Storm* — [NOAA 200 Years: Isaac Cline](https://celebrating200years.noaa.gov/magazine/isaac_cline/welcome.html)
- **Visceral detail [FACT]:** On 10 September a barge carried about 700 bodies (some accounts say almost 1,000) into the Gulf to dump them. Many floated back onto the island's West End. From 11 September the Relief Committee burned bodies on pyres made from wreckage, because the high water table made burial impossible. The fires burned for weeks — [Galveston & Texas History Center, Storm FAQs](https://www.galvestonhistorycenter.org/research/storm-faqs); [History Hit](https://www.historyhit.com/the-great-galveston-hurricane-the-deadliest-natural-disaster-in-the-history-of-the-united-states/)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the Cuban telegrams had not been blocked?
- **Contrarian twist:** Viewers will assume that more warning would have saved the city. The harder truth is that the warning existed and was blocked by national pride and bureaucracy, and the local office needed Washington's approval to issue warnings. The deeper twist: the city survived because it then raised its entire grade and built a seawall, an engineering response rather than a forecasting one. **[FACT]** for the ban and the dispute over the forecast. **[DEBATE]** for how many lives warnings would have saved. The seawall and grade-raising are commonly documented but were not fetched here (GAP).
- **Ordinary POV:** a telegraph operator at the Galveston office, a dockworker, or an orphanage nurse. Note: the St Mary's Orphanage loss is widely cited but was not verified this session.
- **Communication angle:** the warning travelled by telegraph and was stopped by policy, not physics.
- **Then-and-now:** politicised science, and the debate over disaster alerts and "who is allowed to warn".

#### Candidate 3: Napoleon's retreat from Russia (1812). It wasn't (only) "General Winter".
- **Real event [FACT]:** A mass grave of Grande Armée soldiers, dating from the 1812 retreat, was found in Vilnius during construction in 2001 — [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/dna-reveals-what-killed-napoleons-soldiers-during-their-disastrous-retreat-from-russia-in-1812); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/cold-hungry-and-thirsty-napoleons-troops-also-suffered-from-several-diseases-as-they-retreated-from-russia-180987579/)
- **A 2006 study (Raoult et al., *J. Infectious Diseases*) [FACT]:** found body-louse fragments in the grave soil. Of 35 soldiers' dental pulp, 7 had DNA of *Bartonella quintana* (trench fever) and 3 had *Rickettsia prowazekii* (typhus). The study concluded that louse-borne disease affected nearly a third of those tested — [HAL archive](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00008119v1)
- **A 2025 study (Barbieri et al., *Current Biology*) [FACT/DEBATE]:** found *Salmonella enterica* (paratyphoid/enteric fever) and *Borrelia recurrentis* (louse-borne relapsing fever) in 13 soldiers, and no typhus or trench fever. The authors say the small sample does not rule typhus out — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1102129); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/cold-hungry-and-thirsty-napoleons-troops-also-suffered-from-several-diseases-as-they-retreated-from-russia-180987579/)
- **Visceral detail [FACT]:** fragments of actual body lice were recovered from the soil around the skeletons — [HAL archive](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00008119v1)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if Napoleon had stopped at Vilnius or Smolensk in summer?
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief says the Russian winter destroyed the army. Evidence shows lice-borne and gut infections, hunger and exhaustion as major killers. The skeletons carry the DNA of lice and fevers, not "cold". **[FACT]** for the pathogen findings. **[DEBATE]** for which pathogen dominated. The claim that losses were heavy before the frost is commonly made but was not sourced this session (GAP).
- **Ordinary POV:** a teenage drummer boy, a vivandière (camp-follower woman), or a Lithuanian townsperson in Vilnius receiving the returning soldiers.
- **Communication angle:** Paris received news by courier and through official "bulletins" that softened the disaster. The bulletin details were not verified this session (GAP).
- **Then-and-now:** disease rather than combat as the hidden killer; ancient-DNA science rewriting history.

#### Candidate 4: Mosquitoes sold Louisiana. Saint-Domingue/Haiti (1802–1803).
- **Real event [FACT]:** Napoleon sent General Leclerc to retake Saint-Domingue in 1802. By May 1802 Leclerc reported 1,200 hospitalised and more than 30 deaths a day. Leclerc himself died of yellow fever on 2 November 1802 — [NPS, "Mosquitoes contribute to Louisiana Purchase"](https://www.nps.gov/articles/mosquitoes-contribute-to-louisiana-purchase.htm); [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/leclerc-charles-victor-emmanuel-1772-1802)
- The expedition was also meant to secure Louisiana. Its failure contributed to Napoleon's decision to sell Louisiana to the US in April 1803 — [BnF France-Amériques](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-ameriques/en/1802-expedition-saint-domingue-haiti-and-louisiana-purchase); [64 Parishes](https://64parishes.org/entry/saint-domingue-revolution)
- **Communication angle [FACT]:** orders disbanding a reinforcement expedition arrived on 2 May 1803, two days after the Louisiana Purchase treaty was signed in Paris. Paris, the Caribbean and Washington were working on information that was weeks old — [64 Parishes](https://64parishes.org/entry/saint-domingue-revolution)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the French had arrived in the dry season, or yellow fever had been weaker that year?
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief: the US "bought" half a continent through clever diplomacy (Jefferson). Evidence: the sale was made possible by a Black revolution plus a mosquito-borne virus that destroyed a French army. The giant real-estate deal was a by-product of Haitian resistance. **[FACT]** that the failure contributed. **[DEBATE]** for how decisive it was: the BnF and academic sources state the link more cautiously than popular ones.
- **Important nuance:** do not reduce the Haitian victory to "mosquitoes did it". Haitian military strategy, including drawing the French into the fever season, is part of scholarship (J.R. McNeill, *Mosquito Empires*; not fetched, GAP). Present disease plus resistance together.
- **Ordinary POV:** a 19-year-old French conscript writing home, or a formerly enslaved soldier in Dessalines's army.
- **Visceral detail:** GAP. Yellow fever's "black vomit" (vómito negro) is a standard clinical term but was not sourced this session. Cite the CDC or WHO.
- **Then-and-now:** climate and mosquito-borne disease (dengue) — highly resonant in Vietnam, where dengue is familiar to viewers.

#### Candidate 5: Krakatoa (26–27 August 1883). The first "global breaking news" disaster.
- **Real event [FACT]:** Of about 36,000 deaths, more than 34,000 are attributed to tsunamis, not to the explosion itself — [NOAA NCEI](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/day-historic-krakatau-eruption-1883). Other counts give 36,417 or about 37,000 (DEBATE).
- **Communication angle [FACT]:** Anjer (Java), the local telegraph station, sent many of the eyewitness accounts. News went by cable from Batavia to Singapore and then around the world, and London knew within hours — [Royal Society Te Apārangi / Papers Past archive](https://dynamic-rsnz.natlib.govt.nz/newspapers/DTN18831023.2.20); [Branch Collective essay (Monique Morgan)](https://branchcollective.org/?p=1980). Anjer itself was wiped out by the waves — [History.com](https://www.history.com/topics/natural-disasters-and-environment/krakatoa)
- **Visceral detail [FACT, details DEBATE]:** For up to a year afterward, pumice rafts carried human skeletons across the Indian Ocean to East Africa. Winchester (*Krakatoa*) describes a Zanzibar find of pumice carrying human, monkey and possibly tiger skeletons — [EBSCO Research Starters](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/krakatoa-volcano-erupts); [Futility Closet](https://www.futilitycloset.com/2026/02/20/casualties/). The tigers and exact distances come from popular retellings, so treat them with caution.
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the eruption had happened 30 years earlier, before the undersea cables? The world would have learned months later, if at all.
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief: lava and the explosion killed the victims. Evidence: the sea killed them, and most never saw the volcano. A second twist: the telegraph made the disaster global, and the same telegraph clerk who sent the news was in the zone of the waves. **[FACT]** for the tsunami share.
- **Ordinary POV:** the telegraph clerk at Anjer, or a Lloyd's agent. This is fictionalised. Do not attribute words to real named clerks without a source.
- **Then-and-now:** livestreamed disasters; tsunami warnings (2004, 2018 Anak Krakatau — [SCMP](https://scmp.com/news/asia/southeast-asia/article/2179451/tsunami-has-killed-hundreds-krakatoas-massive-eruption-1883)). The setting is Southeast Asia, so it is close to home for Vietnamese viewers.

#### Candidate 6: The 1858 Atlantic cable. The internet that died in three weeks.
- **Real event [FACT]:** The first transatlantic telegraph cable was completed in August 1858. Queen Victoria's 98-word message took about 16 hours to transmit (some sources say 17). The last complete message came on 1 September 1858, so the cable worked for about three weeks — [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/first-transatlantic-telegraph-cable); [National MagLab](https://nationalmaglab.org/magnet-academy/history-of-electricity-magnetism/museum/transatlantic-telegraph-cable-1858/); [Ingenium Canada](https://ingeniumcanada.org/channel/innovation/first-transatlantic-telegraph-cable-1858)
- Chief electrician Wildman Whitehouse applied up to about 2,000 volts and the insulation failed. An inquiry blamed him, but later analysis points to faulty manufacture, storage and handling that would have doomed the cable anyway — [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/28330/how-wildman-whitehouse-destroyed-transatlantic-cable); [National MagLab](https://nationalmaglab.org/magnet-academy/history-of-electricity-magnetism/museum/transatlantic-telegraph-cable-1858/)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the cable had survived? A durable link was not achieved until 1866, an 8-year gap. That date is common knowledge but not fetched here (GAP).
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief: one arrogant engineer fried the cable. Evidence: the cable was already badly made and was going to fail. The scapegoat story is simpler than the systemic truth. **[DEBATE]** between the inquiry and later engineering analysis.
- **Ordinary POV:** a telegraph operator at Valentia (Ireland) or Trinity Bay (Newfoundland), or a sailor on the cable ship.
- **Communication angle:** the core theme. Before the cable, a message took about 10 days by ship, versus hours by cable. Then it went dark again — [Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/mte-in-1858-engineers-laid-the-first-telegraph-cable-across-the-atlantic-it-worked-for-only-three-weeks-before-failing-but-it-cut-the-message-time-between-london-and-new-york-from-ten-days-to-a-matter/) (popular source)
- **Visceral detail:** GAP (none found). Possibly use the gutta-percha insulation from Malaysian trees, which has Southeast Asian relevance, but this needs a source.
- **Then-and-now:** undersea internet cables (Vietnam's frequent AAG/APG cable breaks slow the internet — highly relatable; check current news before citing).

#### Candidate 7: Scurvy and the Royal Navy (1740s–1795). The cure that sat on a shelf for 48 years.
- **Real event [FACT]:** In 1747, aboard HMS Salisbury, James Lind tested six treatments. Only the pair given citrus recovered rapidly — [USNI Naval History](https://usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/february/finding-cure-scurvy); [BBC](https://bbc.in/2ErAEXg)
- Lind did not clearly declare citrus the definitive cure in his 1753 *Treatise*. Gilbert Blane and Thomas Trotter pushed for adoption. In 1795, the year after Lind's death, the Admiralty made lemon juice compulsory, and by 1797 scurvy had nearly vanished from the fleet — [USNI](https://usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/february/finding-cure-scurvy); [Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-age-of-scurvy). One source gives 1790, but most say 1795 (DEBATE).
- **The lemon-to-lime switch [FACT]:** lemons were later replaced by cheaper West Indian limes — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-the-british-defeated-napoleon-with-citrus-fruit-58826). It is widely argued that limes were less effective, but that point was not verified this session (GAP).
- **Visceral detail [FACT]:** on Anson's voyage (1740–44), scurvy made gums bleed and teeth fall out, reopened old wounds, and re-broke healed fractures along the old seam. Anson returned with 188 of about 1,854 men, most losses to scurvy — [Science History Institute, "The Age of Scurvy"](https://sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-age-of-scurvy); [USNI](https://usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/february/finding-cure-scurvy). The single-source claim that a sailor's 50-year-old Battle of the Boyne wound reopened needs checking against Richard Walter's 1748 voyage account.
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the Navy had adopted citrus in 1753 instead of 1795?
- **Contrarian twist:** Popular belief: Lind discovered the cure and saved sailors. Evidence: Lind himself was unsure, the cure sat unused for about 48 years, and its adoption is linked to British naval endurance in the blockades of the Napoleonic era. The Conversation argues it helped defeat Napoleon (DEBATE). The twist: a discovery is not the same as a solution, and implementation is the hard part.
- **Ordinary POV:** a ship's boy or an able seaman in his 40s whose childhood wound reopens.
- **Communication angle:** knowledge moved slower than ships. Findings sat in a book for decades.
- **Then-and-now:** the "evidence-to-policy" gap in modern medicine; nutrition myths.

#### Candidate 8: The Luddites (1811–1816). They were not against technology.
- **Real event [FACT]:** Frame-breaking began on 11 March 1811 in Arnold, Nottinghamshire, and spread across the Midlands and northern England — [Murray State (Kevin Binfield) Luddite history](https://campus.murraystate.edu/academic/faculty/kBinfield/luddites/LudditeHistory.htm). Parliament made machine-breaking a capital offence in 1812 (the Frame Breaking Act) — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/luddite-industrial-revolution-anti-technology)
- **Contrarian twist [FACT, per Smithsonian/NatGeo]:** The original Luddites were "neither opposed to technology nor inept at using it". Many were skilled machine operators, the machines they attacked were not particularly new, and their target was the wage-cutting system, not machines in general — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/what-the-luddites-really-fought-against-264412/); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/luddite-industrial-revolution-anti-technology)
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if Parliament had negotiated wage rules instead of hanging frame-breakers?
- **Ordinary POV:** a Yorkshire cropper or a Nottingham stockinger (knitter), or a 12-year-old mill child.
- **Visceral detail:** GAP. Child-mill injuries and lint-filled lungs are well documented elsewhere, but no source was fetched.
- **Communication angle:** letters signed by the fictional "General Ludd"/"King Ludd" were sent to factory owners. This is common knowledge but unverified here (GAP).
- **Then-and-now:** AI and job displacement. This is the most directly resonant topic for viewers aged 16–35 (NatGeo itself frames it as "before AI skeptics").

#### Candidate 9: Perry's "Black Ships" (July 1853). Japan was warned a year in advance.
- **Real event [FACT]:** On 5 June 1852 (per a Japanese-wiki compilation), Dutch trading-post head Donker Curtius submitted a "Special News" report to the Nagasaki magistrate about the coming American expedition. In October 1852 senior councillor Abe Masahiro asked the coastal defence office for its view, and in November it answered that the report "seemed dubious" — [Japanese Wiki Corpus, "Arrival of the Black Ships"](https://japanese-wiki-corpus.github.io/history/The%20Arrival%20of%20the%20Black%20Ships.html); [Graduate Institute Geneva paper](https://repository.graduateinstitute.ch/record/299311/files/Bypassing_the_Dutch_Monopoly.pdf); [Navy & Marine Living History](https://www.navyandmarine.org/ondeck/1800perryjapan.htm)
- **Contrarian twist [FACT/DEBATE]:** Popular belief: Japan was a "closed" country, totally surprised by Perry. Evidence: the shogunate had intelligence a year ahead through its Dutch channel at Dejima, and it still under-prepared. The twist is the opposite of "isolation equals ignorance": they knew, and chose not to believe. How far the warning reached the top is debated.
- **Divergence [SPEC]:** What if the 1852 warning had been taken seriously?
- **Ordinary POV:** a Uraga fisherman, or a junior Dutch-language interpreter (Oranda tsūji) in Nagasaki who translates the warning nobody believes.
- **Communication angle:** a warning carried by an annual Dutch ship from Batavia to Nagasaki, then by a slow bureaucratic chain to Edo.
- **Visceral detail:** GAP (none found).
- **Then-and-now:** intelligence failures; "we were warned" stories. Japan's response to the West is a useful Asian comparison without touching Vietnamese politics.
- **Sources caveat:** sources for the 1852 dates are secondary compilations. Verify them with W.G. Beasley or Japanese archival scholarship.

#### Candidate 10: Mount Pelée, Martinique (8 May 1902). The newspaper said "no danger" that morning.
- **Real event [FACT]:** Governor Louis Mouttet formed a commission of local lycée teachers, an artillery officer and a civil engineer, with no volcanologist. It judged the danger negligible. Its report ran in the newspaper *Les Colonies* on the morning of 8 May, the day the eruption destroyed Saint-Pierre. A run-off election was set for 11 May. Mouttet and his wife died in the eruption — [Scientific American, History of Geology](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/may-8-1902-la-pelee/); [Earth Magazine](https://www.earthmagazine.org/article/benchmarks-may-8-1902-deadly-eruption-mount-pelee); [History Today](https://www.historytoday.com/node/46228); [Wikipedia: Louis Mouttet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Mouttet)
- **Survivor [FACT]:** Ludger Sylbaris (also called Louis-Auguste Cyparis) survived in a thick-walled, partly underground stone prison cell. He was found about four days later and later toured with Barnum & Bailey's circus. Léon Compère-Léandre also survived, and the exact number of survivors is debated — [Wikipedia: Ludger Sylbaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludger_Sylbaris); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-prison-cell-of-the-man-who-lived-through-doomsday-5524012)
- **Visceral detail [FACT]:** Sylbaris suffered severe burns. The three other rescued residents died of their burns soon after — [Wikipedia: Ludger Sylbaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludger_Sylbaris)
- **Contrarian twist [DEBATE]:** Popular belief: a corrupt governor kept people in town for an election. Evidence: the election motive is an interpretation, and documentation is thin. Sources also note that frightened country people fled *into* Saint-Pierre, believing it safer. The twist: the "safe" big city was the trap, and the only protected person was the prisoner nobody valued — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/history-of-geology/may-8-1902-la-pelee/); [HistoryDaily](https://historydaily.org/1902-mount-pele-eruption-volcano-kills-30000-governor-decides-election-more-important) (popular source that overstates the election angle)
- **Ordinary POV:** the prisoner himself, or a laundress, or a sailor on a ship in the harbour.
- **Communication angle:** the telegraph cable to Fort-de-France went dead. The death toll of roughly 28,000–30,000 is commonly cited but varies (DEBATE).
- **Then-and-now:** expert commissions and official reassurance (COVID-era parallels); "safe zones" that aren't.

### Inferences
- The three communication-speed episodes (Waterloo, Krakatoa, 1858 cable) work as a mini-arc: 3 days → 16 hours → instant global news, all within one lifetime (1815–1883).
- Galveston, Perry and Pelée share the most powerful twist for this format: the warning existed, and humans dismissed it. That suits an ordinary narrator who "knows but cannot convince anyone".
- Haiti and Napoleon's 1812 campaign pair well as "microbes beat empires", and the Vilnius DNA studies offer modern-science credibility.
- For Vietnamese viewers aged 16–35, the most resonant are probably the Luddites (AI and jobs), Krakatoa (Southeast Asia, tsunamis), Haiti (dengue and mosquitoes), Perry (Asian modernisation without touching Vietnam), and the 1858 cable (undersea cable breaks). This is an inference, not audience data.

### Gaps
- Visceral details still unsourced for Waterloo, Haiti, the cable, the Luddites and Perry (see each candidate).
- The Galveston seawall and grade-raising, the claim that Napoleon's losses came before the frost, Lind's limes being less effective, and the 1866 cable were not fetched. They are well known but need a citation.
- Krakatoa's tiger skeletons and exact drift distances come from popular retellings of Winchester. Check the original.
- No audience data was gathered on Vietnamese viewers. The appeal reasoning is inference.
- Not researched despite being strong candidates: the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak (John Snow; removing the pump handle came after the epidemic was already declining), Lisbon 1755, Jenner versus the farmer Benjamin Jesty (1774), the Franklin Expedition of 1845, the Irish Famine, and Sarajevo 1914 (the driver's wrong turn).

## Key Question 2: Where is popular belief wrong or oversimplified in a way that makes a strong reversal?

### Takeaway
Six candidates have a well-sourced reversal: the Rothschild/Waterloo myth (fabricated in 1846), the Luddites (not anti-technology), Krakatoa (killed by the sea, not by fire), Napoleon in 1812 (lice and fevers, not just winter), Perry (Japan was warned), and scurvy (cure known for about 48 years before use). Galveston, Haiti, the 1858 cable and Pelée have reversals that are real but partly debated, and must be labelled as such on screen.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The safest twists for a channel that must not invent facts are Waterloo, the Luddites, Krakatoa and scurvy, which rest on strong institutional sources.
- On Haiti and Pelée, scripts should say "historians argue" rather than state causation as fact.
- The Waterloo episode needs careful framing to debunk the antisemitic conspiracy without amplifying it.

### Gaps
- No primary sources were read: no ship logs, no *Les Colonies* text, no Wellington dispatch. Primary verification is needed before quoting exact wording.
- The death tolls for Galveston (8,000–12,000), Pelée (about 28,000–30,000) and Krakatoa (36,000–37,000) vary by source. Present them as ranges.
