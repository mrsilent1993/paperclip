# Phát minh tình cờ / suýt bị bỏ lỡ: 10 ứng viên cho "What If…?" (accidental and nearly-missed discoveries)

Labels used throughout: **[FACT]** = established by reputable sources; **[DEBATE]** = sources or historians disagree; **[SPEC]** = speculation suitable only for the "what if" segment of an episode and must be framed as such on screen.
Method note: these notes come from web search results (summaries of the pages listed). Search results summarize pages, so before scripting, open each cited page and confirm any exact quote or number used on screen. Where a claim rests only on my background knowledge and not on a source found in this session, it is listed under Gaps rather than Findings.
Exclusions honoured: no Semmelweis, no Vietnamese politics, and no health advice. Medical episodes must be framed as history, not as guidance.

The strongest are Penicillin (#2), Smallpox/Jesty (#3), X-rays/Clarence Dally (#5), Potato & Maize (#9), and Canning (#8). Each has a well-documented twist and an ordinary person at its centre.

### Cited Findings

## Q2. Candidate 1: Penicillin. "What if the mould had never landed in Fleming's dish, or had been taken seriously in 1929?"

### Takeaway
Fleming's 1928 observation was the easy part. Penicillin became a usable drug only through the Oxford team's work around 1940–41, wartime scale-up in Peoria (USA), and a mouldy cantaloupe from a Peoria market. The "lone genius" story is the myth to bust.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** The first patient treated by the Oxford team was Albert Alexander, a police constable. Supplies ran out, and he relapsed and died on 15 March 1941 — [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-magic-arrow-penicillin-and-the-recurrin-urine-37952); [PBS NewsHour](https://pbs.org/newshour/health/the-real-story-behind-the-worlds-first-antibiotic)
- **[FACT] Gross-but-true detail:** penicillin was so scarce that it was recovered from treated patients' urine and re-used. One account says about 80% of a dose is excreted unchanged. Heatley told the team that more penicillin could be purified from urine than could be produced by cultivation — [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-magic-arrow-penicillin-and-the-recurrin-urine-37952)
- **[FACT]** In summer 1941, Florey and Heatley flew to the USA and worked at the USDA Northern Regional Research Laboratory in Peoria, Illinois — [PBS NewsHour](https://pbs.org/newshour/health/the-real-story-behind-the-worlds-first-antibiotic); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/article/how-world-war-ii-put-penicillin-into-every-pharmacy)
- **[FACT]** A strain cultured from a mouldy cantaloupe from a Peoria market was far more productive than Fleming's mould. Smithsonian says it yielded about six times as much — [The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/how-a-moldy-cantaloupe-took-fleming-s-penicillin-from-discovery-to-mass-production-72304); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/a-most-miraculous-mold-180985449/)
- **[DEBATE]** Who brought the cantaloupe:
  - PBS says lab assistant Mary Hunt brought it in — [PBS](https://pbs.org/newshour/health/the-real-story-behind-the-worlds-first-antibiotic)
  - Smithsonian says Hunt shared the nickname "Moldy Mary", but the true finder was probably an anonymous Peoria woman who handed an old cantaloupe to a lab guard — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/a-most-miraculous-mold-180985449/)
- **[FACT]** At Oxford, women workers known as the "penicillin girls" did the hands-on culturing that made the early trials possible — [Smithsonian, "Penicillin girls"](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/pencillin-girls-made-one-worlds-most-life-saving-discoveries-possible-180985449/)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** (a) the mould never lands in the dish; or (b) Fleming's 1929 paper is acted on a decade earlier, before WWII. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - The myth is "one lucky Scot saved the world".
  - The sourced reality is a team, wartime industrial scale-up, and a fruit-market cantaloupe.
  - The Mary Hunt legend is itself disputed, which gives a second layer of myth-busting.
- **POV character:** a "penicillin girl" at Oxford tending culture vessels, or a nurse collecting urine to recover penicillin for Albert Alexander.
- **Spread/communication:**
  - The 11-year gap between the 1928 observation and the Oxford work shows a discovery that failed to spread because nobody could produce it.
  - War, with government funding and US–UK transfer, made it spread.
- **Then-and-now resonance:** scarce medicines and shortages; scaling a discovery up versus discovering it; team science versus credit to one person.
- **Appeal (16–35 VN):** the cantaloupe-from-the-market twist is a strong hook, and "the hero was a woman nobody remembers" suits Gen Z myth-busting content.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source for Fleming's exact 1928 date or the "open window" detail; verify with the Science History Institute or Nobel material. The "dirty dish" telling is itself contested; it was not verified in this session.
- Exact US production figures for D-Day were not retrieved.

## Q3. Candidate 2: Smallpox inoculation and vaccination. "What if nobody had noticed the milkmaids?"

### Takeaway
Jenner did not invent cowpox protection. Farmer Benjamin Jesty used it in 1774, and modern genomics suggests the vaccine virus lineage may be closer to horsepox than to cowpox.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** In 1774, during a smallpox outbreak, Dorset farmer Benjamin Jesty deliberately infected his wife and two sons with cowpox. He never published, and Jenner, who acted 22 years later and published, received the credit — [Nature Milestones](https://www.nature.com/articles/d42859-020-00007-6); [James Lind Library](https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/?p=1128)
- **[FACT]** On 14 May 1796, Jenner inoculated James Phipps with material from a cowpox lesion on milkmaid Sarah Nelmes (also spelled "Nelms"), and later challenged him with smallpox — [Nature Milestones](https://www.nature.com/articles/d42859-020-00007-6)
- **[FACT/DEBATE]** A 1902 vaccine sample (H.K. Mulford, Philadelphia) was 99.7% similar to horsepox. Researchers say this does not prove Jenner used horsepox, but it is possible; Jenner himself experimented with horsepox — [MinnPost on 2017 study](https://www.minnpost.com/second-opinion/2017/10/scientists-shed-new-light-old-mystery-origins-smallpox-vaccine/); [BMC blog on 19th-c. vaccination kits](https://blogs.biomedcentral.com/on-biology/2020/11/16/exploring-the-history-of-smallpox-vaccination-with-19th-century-american-vaccination-kits/)
- **[FACT]** Modern smallpox vaccine is vaccinia, which is related to cowpox virus but genetically distinct from it — [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6047259)
- **Gross-but-true detail [FACT]:** the "vaccine" was matter taken from a pus-filled lesion on a milkmaid's hand and placed into cuts on a child's arm — [Nature Milestones](https://www.nature.com/articles/d42859-020-00007-6)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** Jesty's 1774 experiment stays a village secret forever, or Jenner never publishes in 1798. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - The popular myth is "Jenner, lone genius, invented the vaccine from a milkmaid's tip".
  - The twist has three parts: a farmer did it first; the "cow" virus may really be a horse virus; and what made Jenner matter was publishing and campaigning.
  - A good thesis: "communication beat discovery".
- **POV character:** Sarah Nelmes the milkmaid (her hand, her cow), or one of Jesty's sons.
- **Spread/communication:** Jenner's printed publication spread the method; Jesty's undocumented act stayed local.
- **Then-and-now resonance:** credit going to whoever publishes or posts first; science communication; the genomics of old samples.
- **Appeal:** "a cow-to-human, or horse-to-human, story" mixes gross detail with a mystery solved by DNA. Rule for scripting: keep it historical and say nothing about vaccine advice.

### Gaps
- Earlier variolation (Ottoman Empire, China, Onesimus in Boston 1721) was not verified in this session. It would strengthen the "Jenner wasn't first" twist; check NLM or Royal Society sources.
- No sourced information was found on how smallpox vaccination reached Vietnam. It would be a strong local hook, but it is a gap; avoid political framing if pursued.
- The "milkmaids had pretty complexions" folklore was not verified.

## Q4. Candidate 3: Anesthesia. "What if ether had stayed a party drug?"

### Takeaway
Boston's public "Ether Day" (16 Oct 1846) gets the credit. Japan's Hanaoka Seishū performed documented general-anesthesia surgery in 1804, and Crawford Long used ether in 1842. The famous "fastest surgeon" horror story (Liston's 300% mortality) has no contemporary evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** Hanaoka Seishū removed a breast tumour under general anesthesia in 1804, using an oral herbal mixture (including monkshood and thorn apple), not an inhaled one — [Wood Library-Museum](https://www.woodlibrarymuseum.org/history-of-anesthesia/); [Hektoen International](https://hekint.org/2023/08/23/hanaoka-seishu-inventor-of-an-early-general-anesthetic/)
- **[DEBATE]** Some sources date the operation to 1805 rather than 1804 — [Hektoen International](https://hekint.org/2023/08/23/hanaoka-seishu-inventor-of-an-early-general-anesthetic/)
- **[FACT]** Crawford Long used ether on 30 March 1842 to remove a neck tumour, but did not publish until 1849 — [Wood Library-Museum](https://www.woodlibrarymuseum.org/history-of-anesthesia/)
- **[FACT]** On 16 Oct 1846, W.T.G. Morton gave the first successful public demonstration of ether anesthesia for surgery — [Wood Library-Museum](https://www.woodlibrarymuseum.org/history-of-anesthesia/)
- **[FACT/myth-bust]** The tale that Robert Liston had a "300% mortality" operation comes only from Richard Gordon's 1983 book *Great Medical Disasters*, with no contemporary record — [BBC History Magazine via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/bbc-history-magazine/20220609/282230899307167)
- **[FACT]** About 1 in 10 of Liston's patients died, compared with about 1 in 4 at nearby St Bartholomew's — [BBC History Magazine via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/uk/bbc-history-magazine/20220609/282230899307167)
- **[FACT]** The WFSA tutorial covers anesthesia before 1846 — [WFSA, "A short history of anaesthesia before 1846"](https://resources.wfsahq.org/wp-content/uploads/UIA40-A-short-history-of-anaesthesia-before-1846.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** Hanaoka's method spreads beyond Japan in the 1800s (Japan's isolation policy kept it local), so painless surgery arrives 40 years early. Alternatively, Long publishes in 1842. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - The myth is "an American dentist invented anesthesia in 1846".
  - The reality: a Japanese surgeon did it first, and a Georgia doctor did it four years before Morton.
  - Morton won because of the public demonstration and publicity.
  - Second twist: the gory "300%" story people love is itself unsupported.
- **POV character:** Hanaoka's patient (the historically named patient was an elderly woman; confirm her name before use), or a hospital orderly in Boston who held patients down before 1846.
- **Gross-but-true:** pre-anesthesia surgery depended on speed and restraint. Use Liston's sourced real mortality figures rather than the myth.
- **Spread/communication:**
  - Isolation slowed Hanaoka's method; it did not spread.
  - Morton's public show spread ether.
  - Same lesson as the smallpox episode: publicity beats priority.
- **Then-and-now:** who gets credit (East vs West); viral demonstrations; isolation versus open science.
- **Appeal:** an Asian pioneer erased from Western textbooks resonates strongly with Vietnamese audiences.

### Gaps
- The speed of ether's spread (it reportedly reached London by December 1846) was not verified in this session.
- The name and outcome of Hanaoka's patient were not verified.

## Q5. Candidate 4: X-rays. "What if nobody had noticed the glowing screen?"

### Takeaway
Röntgen's 1895 discovery spread worldwide through newspapers in weeks, faster than his scientific paper did. The ordinary worker in the story, Edison's assistant Clarence Dally, paid with both arms and his life.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** Röntgen radiographed his wife Anna Bertha's left hand on 22 Dec 1895. The news broke on 5 Jan 1896 in Vienna's *Die Presse* and was echoed worldwide from 6 Jan — [Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com/i-have-seen-my-death-the-first-x-ray-photograph); [APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2001/11/1895-roentgens-discovery-xrays)
- **[DEBATE]** One study argues that the newspaper article spread X-rays faster than Röntgen's own paper did — [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/x-rays-were-a-life-saving-accident-1259196)
- **[DEBATE]** Anna Bertha's "I have seen my death" quote is widely repeated, but I found no primary source for it. Present it as "reportedly" — [Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com/i-have-seen-my-death-the-first-x-ray-photograph)
- **[FACT]** By mid-January 1896 there was an "X-ray mania" — [NYAM Center for History](https://nyamcenterforhistory.org/tag/wilhelm-rontgen)
- **[FACT]** Clarence Dally, Edison's chief X-ray assistant:
  - He lost his left hand, then parts of his right hand, then both arms.
  - The NYT reported seven operations.
  - He died in October 1904 and is considered the first US death from X-ray exposure.
  - Edison abandoned X-ray research afterward and kept Dally on the payroll.
  - Sources: [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/clarence-dally-the-man-who-gave-thomas-edison-x-ray-vision-123713565/); [AuntMinnie Europe](https://www.auntminnieeurope.com/clinical-news/digital-x-ray/article/15653614/was-clarence-dally-a-radiology-martyr-or-victim); [Wikipedia (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Madison_Dally)
- **[FACT/caution]** Some sources say Dally was on standby with X-ray equipment in Buffalo after the McKinley shooting; that was actually his brother Charles — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Madison_Dally)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** Röntgen ignores the glowing barium platinocyanide screen, or the press doesn't run the story. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - The myth is "X-rays were an instant pure blessing".
  - The reality is a media frenzy, followed by casualties among the workers who tested the rays.
  - The "martyr" label for Dally is debated: martyr or victim? (see the AuntMinnie article)
- **POV character:** Clarence Dally, glass-blower and lab assistant, ideal as an ordinary person. Alternatively Anna Bertha.
- **Gross detail:** progressive amputations of both arms from radiation ulcers (sourced above).
- **Spread/communication:** a newspaper scoop beat peer review, and an image (the skeletal hand) went "viral" in 1896.
- **Then-and-now:** viral images and hype cycles; new tech adopted before safety is known; the 1896 X-ray craze resembles today's AI or crypto hype.
- **Appeal:** body-horror, a viral-image story, and the moral question of who pays for progress.

### Gaps
- Whether Philipp Lenard, William Crookes or others saw fogged plates before Röntgen and missed the discovery was not verified in this session.

## Q6. Candidate 5: Vulcanised rubber. "What if Goodyear's rubber had never hit the hot stove?"

### Takeaway
The "Goodyear hot stove" story credits a mid-19th-century American with vulcanisation. Mesoamerican peoples were processing latex with morning-glory juice, which contains sulfur compounds that cross-link the latex, around 3,000 years earlier.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** Rubber balls from Mesoamerica date to around 1600 BC, more than 3,000 years before Goodyear — [MIT News 1999](https://news.mit.edu/index%2Ephp/1999/rubber); [European Rubber Journal](https://www.european-rubber-journal.com/article/2079519/mesoamericans-vulcanised-rubber-3000-years-before-goodyear)
- **[FACT]** Hosler, Burkett and Tarkanian (*Science*, 1999) showed that latex mixed with morning-glory vine juice yields processed rubber, and that the vine contains sulfur compounds capable of cross-linking latex polymers — [MIT News 1999](https://news.mit.edu/index%2Ephp/1999/rubber)
- **[FACT]** MIT's 2010 follow-up found that different recipes gave different products:
  - a 50:50 blend gave the most bounce (balls);
  - a 3:1 latex-to-morning-glory mix gave the most durable material (sandals);
  - pure latex worked best for bands and adhesives.
  - Source: [MIT News 2010](https://news.mit.edu/2010/mayaball-0524.html)
- **[DEBATE]** Researchers caution that this shows the know-how was available but does not prove the exact recipes used. Ancient rubber is too degraded to test, and no rubber sandals have been found — [MIT News 2010](https://news.mit.edu/2010/mayaball-0524.html)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** Europeans learn the Mesoamerican method in the 1500s, so elastic, durable rubber arrives three centuries early. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:** "Ancient engineers did it 3,000 years before the patent." This is a debate-level claim and should be labelled carefully on screen.
- **POV character:** a Mesoamerican ballgame rubber-maker, or a worker in an 1830s rubber factory whose goods melt in summer.
- **Then-and-now:** indigenous knowledge versus patents; Vietnam is today a major rubber producer (cây cao su). Keep that mention economic and neutral, and avoid colonial-plantation politics.
- **Appeal:** "người xưa đã làm trước" (the ancients did it first) is reliably popular; ballgame and sandal imagery are visual.

### Gaps
- Goodyear's 1839 hot-stove anecdote, his debt and prison stays, and Thomas Hancock's British patent (1843–44) were **not verified** in this session. Verify them before using; Science History Institute is a good start.
- No sourced gross detail was found. Candidate (unverified): the stench of rotting early rubber goods in summer.

## Q7. Candidate 6: Chlorinated drinking water. "What if Jersey City hadn't gambled in 1908?"

### Takeaway
Continuous municipal chlorination in the US began at the Boonton Reservoir for Jersey City on 26 Sept 1908. Health officer John L. Leal did it under court pressure, without a patent, and was attacked for "poisoning" the water. The hero is an obscure official, not a famous scientist.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** As sanitary adviser, Leal's duties included clearing illegal privies and other sewage sources from the watershed above the reservoir — [Wikipedia: John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal)
- **[FACT]** A 1908 court opinion found that water unfit to be called "pure and wholesome" reached Jersey City two to three times a year — [Wikipedia: John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal)
- **[DEBATE]** The exact date of the court opinion is given as 1 May or 8 May 1908 — [Wikipedia: John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal)
- **[FACT]** The chlorination system, designed by George Warren Fuller, added chloride of lime. Operations began on 26 Sept 1908, run by George A. Johnson. It was the first continuously chlorinated US municipal supply. Leal did not patent it — [Wikipedia: John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal); [Drexel Q&A with Michael McGuire](https://drexel.edu/news/archive/2014/march/michael-mcguire-q-and-a)
- **[FACT]** Science History Institute's "Distillations" podcast episode covers the experiment — [Science History Institute, "Jersey City's Secret Water Experiment"](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/distillations-pod/jersey-citys-secret-water-experiment/)
- **[FACT]** Chlorination history resource — [ASDWA](https://asdwa.org/2018/09/26/history-of-chlorination)
- **Gross-but-true detail [FACT]:** privies and sewage in the watershed fed directly into the city's drinking water source — [Wikipedia: John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** the court rejects chemical treatment and orders only expensive sewer works, delaying chlorination's spread by years. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - Viewers assume clean water came from big engineering projects or famous scientists.
  - The reality is an unpatented, legally risky chemical "hack" by an obscure health officer, which the public initially distrusted as "poison".
- **POV character:** a waterworks labourer shovelling chloride of lime at Boonton, or a Jersey City mother boiling water.
- **Spread/communication:** the method was presented to the American Water Works Association in June 1909. Leaving it unpatented let it spread freely (per the Wikipedia summary).
- **Then-and-now:** public fear of "chemicals in water" versus evidence; open-source innovation.
- **Appeal:** "the man who poisoned a city to save it" is a strong title hook.

### Gaps
- Before-and-after typhoid death counts for Jersey City were not verified. Sources give conflicting rates (21.4 versus up to 80 per 100,000), so do not put a number on screen without the primary report.
- Avoid any framing that could be read as water-treatment advice.

## Q8. Candidate 7: Canned food. "What if Napoleon's prize had never been won?"

### Takeaway
Appert's 1810 bottling method worked about 50 years before anyone (Pasteur) knew why. The can opener came decades after the can, and the famous "lead from tins killed the Franklin expedition" story has been challenged by newer studies.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** In 1810, Parisian confectioner Nicolas Appert won Napoleon's prize for preserving food by sealing it in glass and heating it in boiling water — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/father-canning-knew-his-process-worked-not-why-it-worked-180961960/); [Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Nicolas_Appert)
- **[FACT]** Appert knew his process worked but not why it worked — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/father-canning-knew-his-process-worked-not-why-it-worked-180961960/)
- **[DEBATE]** Can openers came about 30 to 50 years after the can (Yeates 1855 or Warner 1858; sources conflict) — [Food Quality & Safety](https://www.foodqualityandsafety.com/article/peter-durands-metal-can-led-to-a-food-safety-staple/); [Almanac](https://almanac.com/fact/who-invented-the-tin-canr-canning-was)
- **Gross/visceral detail [FACT]:** early cans had to be opened with a chisel, or by troops with a bayonet — [Almanac](https://almanac.com/fact/who-invented-the-tin-canr-canning-was)
- **[FACT]** Franklin's 1845 expedition carried lead-soldered tins, and 1980s studies blamed lead from the cans for the disaster — [Wikimili: Canning](https://wikimili.com/en/Canning)
- **[DEBATE] Franklin lead, first challenge:** a 2013 Western University study argued the voyage was too short for tin lead to dominate the bones, so the exposure was likely pre-voyage — [Western News 2013](https://news.westernu.ca/2013/04/western-researchers-acquit-tins-in-expeditions-fate/)
- **[DEBATE] Franklin lead, second challenge:** a 2014 *Polar Record* study found crew lead levels consistent with 19th-century Britain — [Glasgow University](https://www.gla.ac.uk/colleges/scienceengineering/news/archive/headline_302305_en.html); [ArcticToday](https://www.arctictoday.com/?p=16369)
- **[DEBATE] Franklin lead, third challenge:** a nail study of John Hartnell found normal lead levels until his final weeks — [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/57176-what-doomed-franklin-polar-expedition.html)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** no preservation method by the 1810s, which limits long voyages, polar exploration, and army logistics. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - Myth 1: someone understood the science; they didn't, for about 50 years.
  - Myth 2: Franklin's men were poisoned by their cans; this is increasingly doubted.
  - Myth 3: you could open a can when canned food arrived; there was no opener for decades.
- **POV character:** a sailor or soldier hacking a tin open with a bayonet, or Appert's kitchen hand in Paris.
- **Spread/communication:** a state prize (Napoleon) created the incentive; the British quickly moved to tin cans (Durand).
- **Then-and-now:** a technology used before it is understood (as with some AI today); instant noodles and canned food culture in Vietnam (mì gói, đồ hộp).
- **Appeal:** "they had cans but no can opener for decades" is a punchy, shareable fact.

### Gaps
- The "1939 can opened and still edible" claim came only via secondary search snippets; verify it before use.
- The exact date of the 2018 PLOS One paper was not confirmed.

## Q9. Candidate 8: Printing. "What if movable type had stayed in Asia / what if Gutenberg had failed?"

### Takeaway
Movable type was not a European first. Bi Sheng (around the 1040s) and Korea's metal-type *Jikji* (1377) predate the Gutenberg Bible. Vietnam had its own woodblock-printing founder, Lương Như Hộc (15th century). The more interesting question is why print "exploded" in Europe and not in East Asia.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** *Jikji* was printed with movable metal type in July 1377 at Heungdeok Temple, Cheongju. It is the oldest extant book printed with movable metal type, was inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in September 2001, and is held at the BnF in Paris — [BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/en/jikji-treasure-world-printing); [Wikipedia: Jikji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jikji)
- **[FACT]** The BnF says *Jikji* predates the Gutenberg Bible by 78 years — [BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/en/jikji-treasure-world-printing)
- **[DEBATE]** Methods differed between Goryeo and Gutenberg — [Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/zgXxwilonG6kIg)
- **[FACT]** Bi Sheng developed ceramic/wooden movable type around 1040 CE (secondary source only) — [My Modern Met](https://mymodernmet.com/korea-moveable-type/)
- **[FACT]** Vietnam: Thám hoa Lương Như Hộc (Hồng Lục village, now Thanh Liễu, Hải Dương) is venerated as the founder of woodblock printing (mộc bản). He reportedly learned the craft on missions to China and taught it to villagers around 1470 — [Viet Nam News](https://vietnamnews.vn/sunday/features/1657442/ancient-woodblock-printing-saved-from-the-chop.html); [Lao Động](https://news.laodong.vn/du-lich/kham-pha/ngoi-dinh-tho-to-nghe-khac-in-moc-ban-o-hai-duong-1440703.html); [Znews](https://znews.vn/luong-nhu-hoc-mo-ra-nghe-in-moc-ban-post1460571.html)
- **[DEBATE]** Lương Như Hộc's birth and death dates (1420–1501) and the date he transmitted the craft vary by source — same sources as above

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** Goryeo metal type spreads west along trade routes in the 1400s. Alternatively, Gutenberg's venture fails financially. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - The myth is "Gutenberg invented printing".
  - The reality is that Asia was first, and Europe's advantage was an alphabet of few letters plus market demand.
  - That second point is an **inference / historians' debate**; I did not source it in this session.
- **POV character:** an apprentice woodblock carver in Hồng Lục village, or a typesetter's boy in 1450s Mainz.
- **Spread/communication:** printing is itself a communication technology, so this is a "meta" episode.
- **Then-and-now:** the printing press versus the internet and social media; misinformation spreading through new media.
- **Appeal:** a Vietnamese founder figure (tổ nghề) gives a strong local hook and pride angle that is not political.

### Gaps
- No sourced gross detail was found. One candidate is the claim that printers' ink-balls were dog skin cured in urine; it is **unverified**, so do not use it without a source.
- Gutenberg's bankruptcy and lawsuit with Johann Fust, and the speed of print's spread across Europe, were not verified this session.

## Q10. Candidate 9: Potato & maize reaching Eurasia (and Vietnam). "What if Columbus's ships had carried no seeds?"

### Takeaway
Economists estimate that the potato alone explains about one-quarter of Old World population and urbanization growth from 1700 to 1900. Maize arrived without the Indigenous processing (nixtamalization) that releases niacin, which caused the disease pellagra across poor maize-eating regions. In Vietnam, maize's arrival is credited by legend to Trạng Bùng Phùng Khắc Khoan, a legend without documentary confirmation.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT/DEBATE]** Nunn & Qian (*QJE*, 2011) estimate that the potato accounts for about one-quarter of Old World population and urbanization growth from 1700 to 1900, by their most conservative estimates — [Nunn & Qian PDF](https://nathannunn.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2022/07/nathannunn-the-potatos-contribution.pdf); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:126:y:2011:i:2:p:593-650)
- **[DEBATE]** Replications put the effect between ⅙ and ¼ and flag possible spurious correlation — [Villalobos & Sevilla, critical review](https://whatweowethefuture.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Potatoes-a-critical-review.pdf); [EA Forum replication](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/iZrrWGvx2s2uPtica)
- **[FACT]** Maize not treated with alkali (nixtamalization) is a poor source of niacin and tryptophan. The process was left behind when maize crossed the Atlantic — [Southern Foodways Alliance](https://www.southernfoodways.org/malnourished-cultural-ignorance-paved-the-way-for-pellagra); [Hektoen International](https://hekint.org/2018/07/19/our-daily-bread-the-scourge-of-pellagra/)
- **[FACT]** Goldberger showed pellagra was dietary, not infectious; the later biochemistry was completed by Elvehjem — [TMC Library](https://library.tmc.edu/mcgovern/2014/07/24/texas-medical-center-library-centennial-pellagra-in-a-nutshell/)
- **[DEBATE]** Whether Europeans ignored, dismissed or simply never received nixtamalization is a matter of interpretation — [Southern Foodways Alliance](https://www.southernfoodways.org/malnourished-cultural-ignorance-paved-the-way-for-pellagra)
- **Gross-but-true detail:** pellagra's classic symptoms (skin lesions etc.) — the Hektoen article covers them, but I did not extract exact wording; verify before scripting — [Hektoen International](https://hekint.org/2018/07/19/our-daily-bread-the-scourge-of-pellagra/)
- **[LEGEND]** Vietnamese folk legend: Phùng Khắc Khoan smuggled maize seeds ("ngọc mễ") from Ming China during his 1597 embassy, hiding them past border searches. Search results found this only in press and forum retellings, not in historical documents. Present it explicitly as a legend — [Báo Pháp Luật](https://baophapluat.vn/gian-nan-chuyen-di-su-cua-trang-bung-phung-khac-khoan-post182871.html); [Wikipedia: Phùng Khắc Khoan (does not mention maize)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph%C3%B9ng_Kh%E1%BA%AFc_Khoan)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** New World crops never reach Eurasia, or maize travels together with the nixtamalization method. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist (two layers):**
  - (1) The miracle crop maize carried a hidden disease because the knowledge didn't travel with the seed.
  - (2) Heroic introduction stories are legends: the Parmentier and Frederick the Great potato "guarded field" ruse (not verified this session) and the Phùng Khắc Khoan smuggling tale.
- **POV character:** a Vietnamese farmer planting the first maize in the 1600s (framed with the legend), or a northern Italian peasant eating polenta daily in the 1800s.
- **Spread/communication:** seeds spread fast, but know-how did not. The core lesson is "copying the product without the manual".
- **Then-and-now:** adopting tech or foods without context, such as importing a technology without its usage culture; food security.
- **Appeal:** ngô and khoai are everyday foods in Vietnam, and the Trạng Bùng legend is familiar and fun to "fact-check".

### Gaps
- The Parmentier and Frederick the Great potato legends were not verified this session.
- When sweet potato (khoai lang) and maize actually reached Vietnam, according to academic sources, was not found. The VAAS/IASVN PDFs surfaced but were not read.

## Q11. Candidate 10: Pasteur's chicken-cholera "accident" (1879). "What if the cultures hadn't been forgotten over summer?"

### Takeaway
The textbook story says cultures left untouched over the summer of 1879 lost virulence and became the first lab-attenuated vaccine. Pasteur's private notebooks, studied by historian Gerald Geison, show that his public accounts were misleading. The neat "happy accident" story is a cleaned-up version.

### Cited Findings
- **[FACT]** Cultures of chicken-cholera bacteria sat unused over the summer of 1879, then failed to kill chickens and protected them against fresh virulent cultures — [Nature Milestones](https://www.nature.com/articles/d42859-020-00008-5); [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/scientific-bios/historical-profile-louis-pasteur)
- **[DEBATE]** Who forgot the cultures:
  - Nature says an assistant forgot to inoculate the chickens before leaving — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d42859-020-00008-5)
  - Other accounts have Pasteur himself leaving them during his vacation — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/scientific-bios/historical-profile-louis-pasteur)
  - Attribution to Charles Chamberland appears in low-authority sources only; it is unconfirmed.
- **[FACT]** Geison's *The Private Science of Louis Pasteur* (1995), based on 144 notebooks donated to the Bibliothèque Nationale in 1964, argued Pasteur gave misleading accounts of key discoveries. Pasteur had told his family in 1878 never to show the notebooks — [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/88717); [LRB review](https://lrb.co.uk/v17/n19/arthur-goldhammer/grumpy)
- **[FACT]** Per the Science History Institute, the notebooks show that Pasteur attenuated a bacterium chemically with potassium bichromate, contrary to his public oxygen-attenuation account. In context this was the anthrax vaccine; verify the wording against the page — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/scientific-bios/historical-profile-louis-pasteur)
- **[DEBATE]** Cadeddu (1985) calls the accident story a "vulgate". He argues there was no crucial experiment in fall 1879, that Roux played a major role, and that the attenuation method was unclear to Pasteur until 1880 — [Cadeddu 1985 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/biology/1985-cadeddu.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Divergence point:** the assistant remembers to inoculate the chickens before the holidays, and attenuation is not stumbled upon in 1879. **[SPEC]**
- **Contrarian twist:**
  - "Lucky accident plus genius" is the myth.
  - The notebooks reveal a messier, more collaborative, partly concealed process.
  - The reality is a team (Roux, Chamberland) plus selective public storytelling.
- **POV character:** the junior lab assistant (unnamed in Nature) who returns from summer leave to find old flasks.
- **Gross-but-true:** dead and dying chickens in the lab; injecting birds with weeks-old broth cultures. These are generic and need a specific sourced detail before use.
- **Spread/communication:** Pasteur was a master of public demonstration (Pouilly-le-Fort, 1881) and of controlling his narrative; the hidden notebooks are the "director's cut".
- **Then-and-now:** scientists' and influencers' curated public narratives versus private reality; reproducibility.
- **Appeal:** "the secret notebooks the family was told never to show" is a strong mystery hook. Keep it historical, with no vaccine advice.

### Gaps
- The Chamberland attribution is unconfirmed.
- The Cadeddu article was seen only at summary level. The exact Geison claims about chicken cholera versus anthrax need checking against the Science History Institute page or Geison's book.
